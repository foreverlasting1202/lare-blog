Notes on learning from data

A model can remember a fact and still struggle to work out an answer. We want both: knowledge it can recall, and reasoning it can use to solve a problem. With a fixed training budget, how do we help it learn both?

Think of the distinction as Knowledge (Memorization) vs Reasoning: retaining information from what you read, and working through a problem using what you know. These abilities work together. The training question is how to develop one without losing the other.

Let’s make that concrete with two piles of data: ordinary text for broad knowledge, and worked math solutions as our reasoning examples. We train a roughly 1.5-billion-parameter model on 26.25 billion text tokens and 3.75 billion tokens of solutions. That is a 30-billion-token budget, split 7:1. We will call the streams K (knowledge) and R (reasoning).

Mixing both streams throughout training is a natural starting point. Teaching one first, then the other, is another. To understand what else we can change, we will follow the examples through a training step: which ones arrive, how their losses combine, and how the resulting gradient changes the model.

Start at the switch ↓

01 Learning is not permanent

For the first run, we finish the reasoning examples before switching to ordinary text. At the switch, the model answers 18.50% of GSM8K questions correctly. Will it keep that ability as it learns more knowledge?

We track reasoning with GSM8K word problems and broad knowledge with MMLU questions across many subjects. Both tests draw on recall and reasoning; they give us two useful views of the model rather than a clean separation of abilities.

Knowledge training updates the same parameters that learned to reason through the problems. Once the reasoning examples disappear, the objective no longer asks those updates to preserve that skill. Reversing the order changes which ability loses ground: in the knowledge-first run, MMLU falls during the final reasoning stage even as GSM8K improves.

The model does not put each newly learned subject into a separate drawer. A training schedule also decides how long a skill has to survive while the model is learning something else.

Mixing gives the model repeated opportunities to retain knowledge and practice reasoning. The same reasoning examples are spread across the run. If you want a model to keep using a skill, keep giving it chances to practice that skill while it learns the next one.

We now have both subjects present. The next question is how much each should count.

02 Give reasoning more weight

A language model learns by predicting the next token. The loss measures how much probability it gave the token that actually came next. Giving the correct token very little probability produces a large loss. Training combines these losses and uses their gradients to update the model.

From a prediction to the batch loss

qk=−logpθ(xk|x<k)

Here xk is the observed next token, x<k is its preceding context, and θ denotes the model parameters. Averaging these token losses within each domain gives ℓi.

L=∑iπiwiℓi
πi
Fraction of scored prediction targets from domain i; these shares sum to one.
ℓi
Mean unweighted loss on those targets.
wi
Coefficient applied to that domain’s loss. Equal weighting sets every coefficient to one.

Consider a toy batch with seven knowledge-text predictions and one reasoning-example prediction, all scored. Write their domain mean losses as ℓK and ℓR. We can give the reasoning prediction twice the weight of a knowledge prediction while keeping the average coefficient at one.

Equal weights

L1=7ℓK+ℓR8

Reasoning counts twice as much

L2=7ℓK+2ℓR9

The denominator is 9 because the eight targets now carry 7+2 units of relative weight. In the batch-loss formula above, this corresponds to wK=8/9 and wR=16/9. Their target-weighted mean is one, and the relative ratio is wR/wK=2. The examples and the 7:1 target count have stayed fixed.

Keep the stream fixed, train with several reasoning weights, and put the final models on a chart. Right means better reasoning performance on GSM8K. Up means better knowledge performance on MMLU. Moving up and right would mean improving both.

Moving from 1× to 2× takes us up and right. Reasoning improves while knowledge performance also rises. The change comes from how the existing examples enter the training objective. We have not added another worked solution.

Keep turning the weight up and the pattern changes. At 7×, reasoning improves further, but MMLU is below its value at 2×. Now we are exchanging some knowledge performance for reasoning. The interesting part is that the equal-weight starting point left room to improve both.

03 Is a bigger weight just more data?

At first glance, doubling the reasoning loss weight sounds like showing the model twice as many reasoning examples. Both give reasoning more influence. But sampling more reasoning data changes the batch and, with a fixed token budget, leaves less room for knowledge text. Raising the weight makes the reasoning examples already in the batch count more.

A small example makes the distinction concrete. Reading two independently sampled solutions gives two observations to learn from. Reading one solution with twice the weight still gives one observation. Its quirks get amplified along with its useful signal. This matters when updates are estimated from finite batches.

For a compact comparison, consider an SGD update at the same model parameters, using a batch of B independent examples. Let mi be the fraction drawn from domain i, and g^i(n) its average gradient estimated from n examples. A gradient is the derivative of the loss with respect to the model parameters; η is the learning rate.

Change the sampled fractions to m′

Δθ=−η∑imi′g^i(Bmi′)

Keep the fractions m and change the weights w

Δθ=−η∑imiwig^i(Bmi)

In the first line, mi changes in two places, including inside the sample count. In the second, wi multiplies the gradient estimated from the existing examples. Even choosing mi′=miwi, when those fractions sum to one, only matches the expected SGD update. It leaves different sample counts and therefore different gradient noise. AdamW and Muon further transform the gradients; these two lines describe the SGD case.

The gray points below change the data allocation from 7:1 toward 1:1. The colored points keep an allocation fixed and change the loss weight. They show the models reached by those two choices under the same total training budget.

From a 3:1 mix to a 1:1 mix, the gray path moves right and down: more reasoning data comes with lower knowledge performance. At the fixed 3:1 mix, doubling the reasoning weight moves right and slightly up. Choosing the data ratio still leaves another way to adjust what the model learns.

These controls answer different practical questions. The mixture decides which examples get a place in the budget. The weights decide how strongly the predictions on those examples enter the loss. It is useful to be able to adjust either one without automatically committing to the other.

There is a catch. Each dot required a training run. A good weight found this way costs a sweep, and the answer may change when the model or data changes. Could the losses we already measure during training help choose it?

04 Which predictions need more weight?

A tempting rule is to give more weight to whichever subject has the larger loss. That sounds like spending more time on a student’s weakest subject. But next-token loss is a measure of predictability, and different kinds of text can be predictable to very different degrees.

A worked solution may contain repeated steps and a narrow set of expressions. Ordinary text can contain names, quotations, and many plausible continuations. A larger loss on one source does not by itself tell us that emphasizing it will improve the skill we care about. And a low loss on worked solutions does not mean the model can solve a new problem correctly.

There is another way to use loss: as a rough clue to the scale of the gradients a domain produces. A small noisy classification problem makes this concrete.

A classifier with noisy labels

Draw a Gaussian input x∼𝒩(0,Id) and give it the clean label y0=sign(u⊤x), where u is a fixed unit vector. Flip each label independently with probability ρ∈(0,1/2) to obtain y. The logistic classifier and its average loss are:

pθ(y=1|x)=11+e−θ⊤x
ℓ(θ)=𝔼x,y[log(1+e−yθ⊤x)]

The learned vector θ sets the classifier’s boundary and confidence; the expectation averages over inputs and label flips.

At the optimum, in the small-noise limit ρ→0 and for fixed dimension d≥2, the root-mean-square gradient grows with the square root of the optimal loss. An inverse-square-root weight offsets that scale. This gives us a rule to try on language models without measuring gradient norms for every domain.

The scaling that motivates the exponent

RMS(wigi)∝wiℓi⋆
wi∝1ℓi⋆

Here gi is a per-example gradient and ℓi⋆ is the optimal loss in the toy model. RMS means root-mean-square magnitude. The scaling suppresses comparable domain constants and dimensions. Training cannot observe ℓi⋆, so we use a smoothed current loss as a practical proxy.

The rule is easy to calculate. Smooth each domain’s recent unweighted losses, take the reciprocal of its square root, and normalize the resulting weights so their average across prediction targets is one. We call this LARE, short for Loss-Adaptive Reweighting under fixed Exposure.

Compute a score, then normalize

ri=1ℓ^i+ε
wi=ri∑jπ^jrj
ℓ^i
Smoothed mean loss before applying any domain weight.
π^i
Smoothed fraction of valid prediction targets; the fractions sum to one.
ε
A small positive constant to keep the denominator away from zero.
∑iπ^iwi=1

Every domain uses the same denominator. It preserves the relative weights while fixing their target-weighted mean. After warmup, statistics from the current step determine the next step’s coefficients.

For our knowledge and reasoning streams, the shared normalization cancels when we divide the two weights. Here, reasoning loss means next-token loss on the worked solutions. This makes the direction of the rule especially clear.

wRwK=ℓ^K+εℓ^R+ε

Knowledge loss 4, reasoning loss 1

wRwK≈41=2

The raw scores are 0.5 and 1, so the reasoning weight is twice the knowledge weight. In our earlier toy batch with seven knowledge targets and one reasoning target, normalization gives weights of about 0.889 and 1.778. Choose “Lower reasoning loss” below and check that the relative weight is 2×.

Notice the direction. Holding knowledge loss fixed, lowering reasoning loss raises its relative weight. The rule compensates for different gradient scales using training losses. Whether that compensation improves reasoning is something we measure separately on held-out problems.

Normalization keeps the average coefficient at one, so increasing the reasoning coefficient also adjusts the knowledge coefficient. It gives us a consistent way to express the balance. The final parameter update still depends on the gradients and the optimizer.

When using the rule in training, average each domain’s unweighted loss over recent batches so that one unusual batch does not swing the weights. Count only predictions that contribute to the loss. This keeps the running statistics aligned with the objective being optimized.

05 Choosing a weight in one run

Now let the rule choose weights throughout a real run. During learning-rate warmup, all coefficients stay at one while loss statistics accumulate. After warmup, the smoothed losses set the weights for the next update. The data mixture stays at 7:1 throughout.

The bottom panel records the reasoning-to-knowledge weight ratio. The upper panels track the two capabilities. On the same time axis, we can watch the rule choose a balance and see what the model learns along the way.

The ratio moves above two after warmup and ends at 2.41. This puts it near the useful region we found by hand. The final scores are 32.24 on MMLU and 39.80 on GSM8K, close to the fixed 2× run’s 32.27 and 38.59.

Use the fixed 2× run as a reference. The question is whether an automatic rule can reach a useful balance without trying several weights in separate runs. That makes adaptation valuable when the cost of a sweep is high; the benchmark curves still tell us whether the chosen balance is useful.

06 What should one update change?

We have followed our examples into a weighted loss. Differentiating it gives a gradient. There is one more decision before the model changes: how should the optimizer turn that gradient into a step?

Look inside one linear layer. Its input x is an activation vector from the preceding computation, and its output is y. Holding the input fixed for this step, changing the weight matrix by ΔW changes the output by:

y=WxΔy=ΔWx

A scalar example makes the issue visible. A weight change of 0.01 gives an output change of 0.01 when the input is 1, but 0.10 when the input is 10. To keep the output change at 0.01 in the second case, the weight should move by only 0.001. The same parameter step can have a very different effect.

ActGD, short for Activation Gradient Descent, takes this view into the optimizer. It uses the scale and correlations of a layer’s inputs to construct an update, then normalizes each output row separately.

Let G=∇WL be the gradient of our weighted loss with respect to this layer’s weights. For n input vectors, collect their uncentered second moment in C:

C=1n∑k=1nxkxk⊤

The diagonal of C records input scales; the off-diagonal entries capture how coordinates vary together. For an invertible C, the basic ActGD step for output row j is:

ΔWj=−ηGjC−1GjC−1Gj⊤+ε

Here η is the learning rate, ε is a small numerical guard, and row j produces one output coordinate. Add these row updates to W.

Read the rule in two pieces. Multiplying by C−1 adjusts for input scales and correlations. The denominator sets each row’s root-mean-square output change, ΔWjCΔWj⊤, to approximately η for a nonzero gradient and negligible ε. This is the matrix version of choosing a smaller weight change when the input is larger.

The implementation smooths gradients and input second moments with exponential moving averages, uses a numerically stabilized approximate inverse, and applies decoupled weight decay. The formula above isolates the geometry of one step. The longer runs below use this row-wise update family for the transformer matrices, token embeddings, and output head.

Now we can place the two choices in order. LARE sets how knowledge and reasoning examples contribute to the gradient G. ActGD uses the layer’s inputs to turn G into a parameter change. We can choose the loss weights and the optimizer together, then check what the model actually learned.

07 Which balance of abilities do you want?

After choosing how to form a gradient and how to take a step, return to the question we started with: what should the model be good at? How much reasoning performance do we want relative to broad knowledge?

Pick one optimizer row below: AdamW, Muon, or ActGD. Within that row, the update rule stays fixed. Each cell compares equal loss weights with LARE at the same data mix and budget; moving across columns changes the mix. This lets us ask what loss weighting adds when the optimizer has already been chosen.

Start with the reasoning scores. Then switch to knowledge. Does improving one ability always cost some of the other, or does the answer depend on the data mix?

For example, at 1:1 with ActGD, the reasoning gain on GSM8K is 4.85 percentage points and the MMLU change is −0.94. That may be a useful exchange for a model built to solve problems. It may be undesirable for one whose main job is broad knowledge recall.

This is why the data mixture remains a meaningful design choice even after adding an adaptive rule. The weight helps determine what the model learns from the available stream. It cannot choose your priorities for you.

08 Does the benefit grow with model size?

So far, we have asked how to learn more reasoning from a fixed training stream. Now change the size of the learner. As models get larger, does adjusting the loss weights still make a difference? The useful comparison is the extra gain from reweighting at each size.

The comparison within each size still asks our original question: what more can this model learn from the same data?

When scaling up a training run, revisit how the loss weights balance knowledge and reasoning. In these experiments, the weighting choice matters most at the largest of the three budgets. A recipe chosen on a small model may miss that opportunity.

09 Three subjects, one training run

The same idea extends naturally to more data sources. Add code alongside knowledge text and worked math solutions, and keep a separate smoothed loss for each source. Each gets its inverse-square-root weight, followed by one shared normalization across all prediction targets.

We can then ask whether the balance helps across several kinds of tasks in a longer run. The comparisons below use a roughly 1.5B-parameter model and 120B or 240B training tokens. The three-domain runs allocate text, math, and code in a 7:0.5:0.5 ratio. Within each pair, the examples and training budget are the same.

Alongside MMLU and GSM8K, MATH tests more difficult mathematical reasoning. HumanEval and MBPP ask the model to write programs that pass tests, adding another view of its ability to solve problems.

At 240B tokens, GSM8K rises from 29.72% to 46.78%, and HumanEval rises from 17.68% to 24.39%. MMLU moves more modestly, from 34.13% to 34.96%. The same training stream has produced a different distribution of capabilities, with gains on all five tests in this comparison.

The larger gains are in reasoning and code, even though these examples occupy the smaller shares of the stream. Knowledge and reasoning need not compete at every point. How strongly the examples shape training matters alongside how many of them the model sees.

What to try in your next run

Start with a question you can answer in one controlled comparison: can the current examples teach more if they contribute differently to the update?

  1. Choose the abilities you want to keep and improve. Pick a small set of knowledge and reasoning evaluations before changing the recipe. They make the trade-off visible and give you a reason to prefer one model over another.

  2. Change one control at a time. Keep the data stream, optimizer and token budget fixed while comparing equal weights with a modest increase in reasoning weight. Look for movement up and right. If knowledge falls as reasoning improves, decide whether that exchange fits the model’s job before turning the weight up again.

  3. Let losses guide weights; let tasks judge learning. When separate weight-search runs are too expensive, compare an adaptive rule with a fixed-weight reference. Follow both domain losses and task scores. A lower loss is useful feedback, but solving more problems is the capability you ultimately want.

Choosing the data determines the opportunities to learn. Choosing the objective and the update determines how the model uses those opportunities. That is where the same training data can still have more to teach.
